{%- comment -%} ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════ THE TOWN SKY — the whole town lives under one sky, on every page. ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── 2026-07-12 (Nate: "the other pages, I thought we were going to give them day/dusk/night/dawn themes, like the P&JCC page? Let's really make that a thing"). Up to now the hour existed in exactly two places: the P&JCC hero's full sky, and a 0.03–0.07 color wash over everything else — technically town-wide, invisible in practice. This is the real thing: a fixed, full-viewport sky sitting BEHIND every page (z-index:-1, so it paints over the flat body color and under every card, header and footer). You see it in the gutters, above and below the content — the town is now a place the pages sit inside, instead of a color they're tinted with. It's STATIC MARKUP, not JS-built: `sky-` and `cloud-` are already on before first paint (see town-weather.html), so the sky is correct on the very first frame with no flash and no layout work. Stacking, and it matters — Nate: "the stars should always fall BEHIND the moon. Unless it's a shooting star": stars (1) → orb, sun or moon (2) → shooting stars (3) → clouds (4) So a shooting star can streak across the moon's face, and cloud can pass in front of the lot. Nothing else may sit between the stars and the orb. PERF (the 2026-07-11 lag rules, which this obeys to the letter): the container is position:fixed + overflow:hidden + contain, so it is capped at the VIEWPORT and never grows with the document. Every moving part animates transform/opacity ONLY — no background-position anywhere, no filter on any wrapper of an animating child. Off entirely under reduce-flourish / prefers-reduced-motion, which leaves the static sky (gradient + orb) exactly as it is. ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════ {%- endcomment -%}

{%- comment -%} 2 · the orb: one element, four looks. The day sun grows rays; the night moon gets a crater shadow. Sits ABOVE the stars, always. {%- endcomment -%} {%- comment -%} ⚑ THE CORONA (2026-08-13, Nate: "the partial and full eclipse look great but can we make it more showy? More prominent?"). It sits immediately BEFORE the orb so it paints behind it at the same z-index — the black disc has to be the front-most thing in the picture or there is no eclipse, only a bright smudge. Two pseudo-elements do the work: ::before is the streamers (a ragged conic gradient masked to a ring, turning once every three minutes), ::after is the DIAMOND RING — the bead of raw photosphere at second and third contact, which is the one frame of an eclipse everybody has seen. Both are `display:none` for the other 29 days of the lunation, so this costs the sky nothing at all until the day it happens. {%- endcomment -%} {%- comment -%} ⚑⚑ THE MOON'S OWN NEIGHBORHOOD (2026-08-19). Nate: *"Can the moon have some faint stars in the near area? Only a moon doesn't fully make it clear what it is upon first glance — or if it's cloudy that day, some faint clouds around the moon, and faintly covering it, to give it a more real effect?"* ⚠⚠ A SIBLING OF THE ORB, NOT A CHILD OF IT, AND THAT IS A PERFORMANCE DECISION. `.ts-orb` carries `filter: drop-shadow(0 0 22px …)` at night. A filter forces its whole SUBTREE to re-rasterize whenever anything inside it changes, so a twinkling star parked under the moon would repaint a 51px blurred disc every frame — a cost with no pixels to show for it. Out here the field tracks the moon by reading the same `--orb-x/--orb-y` the orb reads, so it travels the arc with it and pays nothing. ⚠ z-index 3: ABOVE the orb (2) so the veil can lie across the moon's face, BELOW `.ts-clouds` (4) so a genuinely overcast night still covers everything. ⚠ THE STARS REUSE `.ts-star` ON PURPOSE. They are stars; a second star class would be a second thing to keep in step with the twinkle, the reduced-motion guard and the cloud-cover fades. Only their POSITION is new, and it is in px rather than % so they hold the same distance from the moon at every viewport. {%- endcomment -%}

These run r≈45 to r≈105 with the gaps deliberately unequal and two of them close together, because a real patch of sky is clumpy. {%- endcomment -%} {%- comment -%} The veil — drawn only on a `moon-veiled` night (see the roll in pjcc-time.js and the class in town-weather.html). {%- comment -%} ⚑⚑ THE TERMINATOR, AS A REAL ELLIPSE (2026-08-18, Nate: "the moon looks a bit odd in its waning gibbous"). It used to be a same-size disc of sky slid across the face — and two circles of EQUAL radius can only ever cut a crescent, so every gibbous night for a year drew the wrong figure while the lit AREA underneath was exactly right. 61% lit was rendering as a crescent. The real terminator is a circle seen edge-on, i.e. an ELLIPSE, and an SVG path is the only shape language here that can say that. `clipPathUnits="objectBoundingBox"` puts it in 0-1 space, so it fits the orb at ANY diameter — which is why making the moon larger is now one number in the stylesheet and nothing else. ⚠ ONE INSTANCE, ONE ID. This include is the town sky and the town sky is site-wide; a second copy would duplicate the id and the first one silently wins everywhere. ⚠ `aria-hidden` + zero box: it is a definition, never a picture. ⚠ THE DEFAULT `d` IS A FULL DISC. If the clock never runs, the moon is full rather than gone — a fallback must look like the old thing, never like a hole. {%- comment -%} ⚑ THE AURORA (2026-08-13, "1% northern lights"). One night in a hundred. ⚠ THIS IS THE EXPENSIVE ONE AND IT IS BUILT AROUND THAT. The /pjcc/ audit the same day priced the sky in SCREENS of moving texture: two cloud banks alone are 3.32 of the page's 4.11, while eleven twinkling stars and three shooting stars together are effectively 0.00. Painted AREA is the cost, not the element count — so an aurora drawn the obvious way (a full-bleed blurred gradient) would be the most expensive thing in the sky by a wide margin. So: an arc and its rays in the upper sky only, no `filter`, no `mask`. The shimmer is transform + opacity, which the compositor owns; the soft edges are gradient stops, which are painted once into the layer and then never repainted. ⚑ REBUILT 2026-08-13 (Nate: "they are a bit too vertical and you can't really tell what's going on"). The first version was three tall rectangles with razor-straight left and right edges, which is what he was looking at: not an aurora, three translucent columns. What makes a real one READ is a curved ARC low in the display with RAYS standing up out of it, and every edge soft — so that is what this is now. The arc is one element; the rays are seven, each fanned a few degrees off vertical about its own foot and each brightening in turn left-to-right, which is the traveling wave your eye actually recognizes. Geometry is per-element custom properties so the rules stay one block: --x/--w left+width, --h/--foot the height and where it stands, --lean the fan, --t/--d the cycle. {%- endcomment -%}
